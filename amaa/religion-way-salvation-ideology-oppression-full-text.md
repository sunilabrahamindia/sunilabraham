## Introduction

Religions, as we experience them, play a twofold role in society. Primarily they are symbol-systems of meaning. Religious concepts, myths, beliefs and practices supply us with 'conceptions of a general order of existence' with regard to self, society and the universe. The values and meanings provided by religion enable people to celebrate the joys of their lives, to interpret and shape the events of their time and space, and to cope with the problems of life's sufferings and of death.

On the other side, if we historically look at the origin and development of religions and religious movements, we find both in their origin and later development, that religions as symbol systems of meaning had been influencing the political and economic conditions of their time and in turn had been greatly influenced by them. Religious myths, beliefs and practices as they originated in concrete historical, political and economic conditions often reflect the socio-political and cultural structures of their societies. In other words, these myths, concepts and practices may reflect the conflicts, stresses, strains and power relations resulting from such socio-economic and cultural structures. In short, religions as cultural systems besides being meaning systems, also function as ideologies serving the interests of particular segments within the religious community or to promote the interest of one religious community against another. This is the secondary role religions play in societies which in fact may conflict with the message of universal love central to all religions. History is full of examples of this and they can be taken from all over the world, from our own subcontinent, even from present day situations in the country.

The purpose of this paper is not to raise theoretical issues on how religious symbol systems interlock with the economic and political systems. We are aware of the merits and the problems regarding the theories of great theoreticians like Karl Marx and Max Weber.

The aim of this paper is something very modest and very concrete. It is an attempt to understand how Hindu village religion functions as a cultural system in the context of our common task of building an egalitarian society, particularly in relation to the aspirations of the so-called untouchables for social equality and justice. In other words, what is under observation here is the social role village Hinduism plays in terms of the power relations within the village. We do this by studying the social function of the places of worship, of village festivals and of the rites of passage. In order to do this we should first of all have a fair idea of the power relations existing in the villages which result from socio-economic factors such as land and other forms of wealth, education and employment. We could then look upon the religio-cultural system in order to see whether it generally corresponds to and strengthens the socio-economic factors or whether it is in any way in disharmony with or challenges the power relations resulting from material factors.

The study was done in Sarjapura Hobli of Anekal Taluk, a backward area in Bangalore district. According to their caste the people in this area known as Reddys, Vokkaligas, Nagaratha Shettys, Brahmins, Tigaas, Ganigas, Kumbaras, Acharis, Hajamas, Agasas, Togatas, Holeyas and Madigas. In the first part we give the economic profile of five villages selected on a random basis from the area in order to have a picture of the power relations among the castes resulting from material factors such as land, education and employment. It is based on a survey we have conducted ourselves. The picture of the economic conditions of different caste groups that emerges from this survey is a fair representation of the general situation in the whole area.

In the second part we describe the religio-cultural factors based on observations gathered from 20 villages in the area. The purpose of the study, as we mentioned above, is to understand the social role of these factors in terms of power relations among the castes.

From the above tables we find that 75.9% of total land is in the hands of the Reddy, Vokkaliga and Nagaratha Shetty communities. These communities also possess the maximum number of borewells and pumps, bullock carts, draught animals, milk cows, sheep and goats. Out of the total amount of loan of Rs. 4,52,429, from banks and farmers' co-operative service societies—this well-to-do section of the population has claimed Rs. 2,50,800 (55.43%). Apart from these agricultural forms of wealth, some families among them own power looms, flour mills, lorries, cars and two-wheelers.

Among the Brahmins only two families have land. The other two families support themselves with income from the priesthood and government jobs (cfr table 5 below).

Among the Tigalas, 52 people support themselves with small vegetable gardens, another 24 work also as wage labourers to supplement their income from land, while twenty of them have no land and depend entirely on wage labour. The 45 families of occupational castes like Ganigas and Kumbaras who are carpenters, potters, blacksmiths, goldsmiths, barbers etc., possess the least amount of land (0.9%). Most of them live by their vocational skills (cfr. table 5), others by agriculture and the rest combine agriculture and wage labour.

In the 152 families of Holeyas and Madigas, the so-called untouchables, quite a high number 93 persons, work on their own land and supplement their income with wage labour; 38 persons depend entirely on wage labour and only 26 are fully self-employed on their own farms.

From the above data it is clear that the economic power in the villages rests with the Reddy-Vokkaliga-Shetty communities. The vocational castes of Ganiga, Kumbara etc. depend on and serve these castes. The Tigalas, who grow vegetables, though comparatively poor, are not considered untouchables and have a status as small agriculturists. It is the Holeyas and Madigas who have the general image of agricultural wage labour.

Let us look at the educational and non-agricultural job pattern.

The educational and job patterns generally reflect the economic base of the respective groups. The education and jobs further strengthen the powerful position of the Reddys, Vokkaligas, Nagaratha Shettys and Brahmins. Let us now look at the religio-cultural scene of these villages, the data for which has been collected from twenty villages in Sarjapur Hobli in order to make sure that the religious scene presented here is representative of the area.

## Part II. Religio-Cultural Profile of the Villages

### I. Physical Exclusion of the Holeyas and Madigas from the Village Community

In the twenty villages studied, Holeyas are present in 19 where there are also caste Hindus; one small village is made up only of Holeyas. In the 19 villages, Holeyas live in a segregated area, separate from the rest. Madigas are found in six villages, and they live separate from both the caste Hindus and Holeyas.

In recent years there has been some change in this set-up. In ten villages the Janata Housing Scheme has been implemented. In four of these 10 villages, houses have been allotted without regard to caste. In another four villages Janata houses have been built in a separate area for the Holeyas only. In two villages Madigas have been given Janata houses in a separate area.

In most of the villages the caste Hindus themselves live in particular sectors according to their caste and Brahmins or the landlord castes like Reddy and Vokkaligas are found in the centre of the village. The major exception is a large newly developing village, Dodda Dommasandra, where houses are not segregated according to caste but found mingled.

### II. Devasthanams—Symbols of Caste Power

The physical territorial divide between the caste Hindus and the Holeyas and Madigas is culturally reflected in the nature of the places of worship in these villages. The caste Hindus and the Holeyas and Madigas have their own separate places of worship. These places mirror the power and the powerlessness of these two groups.

In the twenty villages surveyed by us, all the thirty important Devasthanams are places of worship of the caste Hindus, and the Holeyas and Madigas can make offerings to the gods in them only from outside. Twenty out of these thirty Devasthanams have land or other sources of income. 90%, that is, 18 out of 20 of these propertied temples have Brahmins or Lingayats as priests, and the remaining two have a Reddy and a Balajika as priest. There are 3 more Devasthanams where the priests are from castes such as Kumbara, Devanga and Tigala, but these 3 Devasthanams have no property. In all, 25 out of 30 Devasthanams have Brahmins or Lingayats as priests, though there are only a few families of Brahmins in these 20 villages.

The gods worshipped in these Devasthanams are all male gods, except one. They are Anjaneya, Veerabhadraswami, Gopalswami, Vinayaka, Gangadhareshwara, Basaveswara, Iswara, Lakshmi Narayana, Tirumalaswami, Pandurangaswami, names suggesting dominance of Vaishnava tradition. A god, Muneswara, worshipped by the so-called lower castes in their gudis with blood offerings is the principal deity in one of the Devasthanams where he is worshipped with vegetarian offerings. The only principal deity who is a female in Devasthanam is Muthelamma.

In contrast to the Devasthanams, the Holeyas and Madigas have as places of worship the gudis. These gudis are either an anthill or a crude image with or without a small roof over it. Some gudis are in a portion of the priest's dwelling. We have counted 29 such gudis in these 20 villages, belonging either to the Holeyas or Madigas. Generally a mother goddess under various names such as Yellamma, Kaveramma, Gangamma, Maduramma, Dugalamma, Maheswaramma, Pajamma, Dalamma, etc. is worshipped there. The only male god among them is the god Muneswara. Holeyas and Madigas have priests of their own community.
