## Introduction

Religions, as we experience them, play a twofold role in society. Primarily they are symbol-systems of meaning. Religious concepts, myths, beliefs and practices supply us with 'conceptions of a general order of existence' with regard to self, society and the universe. The values and meanings provided by religion enable people to celebrate the joys of their lives, to interpret and shape the events of their time and space, and to cope with the problems of life's sufferings and of death.

On the other side, if we historically look at the origin and development of religions and religious movements, we find both in their origin and later development, that religions as symbol systems of meaning had been influencing the political and economic conditions of their time and in turn had been greatly influenced by them. Religious myths, beliefs and practices as they originated in concrete historical, political and economic conditions often reflect the socio-political and cultural structures of their societies. In other words, these myths, concepts and practices may reflect the conflicts, stresses, strains and power relations resulting from such socio-economic and cultural structures. In short, religions as cultural systems besides being meaning systems, also function as ideologies serving the interests of particular segments within the religious community or to promote the interest of one religious community against another. This is the secondary role religions play in societies which in fact may conflict with the message of universal love central to all religions. History is full of examples of this and they can be taken from all over the world, from our own subcontinent, even from present day situations in the country.

The purpose of this paper is not to raise theoretical issues on how religious symbol systems interlock with the economic and political systems. We are aware of the merits and the problems regarding the theories of great theoreticians like Karl Marx and Max Weber.

The aim of this paper is something very modest and very concrete. It is an attempt to understand how Hindu village religion functions as a cultural system in the context of our common task of building an egalitarian society, particularly in relation to the aspirations of the so-called untouchables for social equality and justice. In other words, what is under observation here is the social role village Hinduism plays in terms of the power relations within the village. We do this by studying the social function of the places of worship, of village festivals and of the rites of passage. In order to do this we should first of all have a fair idea of the power relations existing in the villages which result from socio-economic factors such as land and other forms of wealth, education and employment. We could then look upon the religio-cultural system in order to see whether it generally corresponds to and strengthens the socio-economic factors or whether it is in any way in disharmony with or challenges the power relations resulting from material factors.

The study was done in Sarjapura Hobli of Anekal Taluk, a backward area in Bangalore district. According to their caste the people in this area known as Reddys, Vokkaligas, Nagaratha Shettys, Brahmins, Tigaas, Ganigas, Kumbaras, Acharis, Hajamas, Agasas, Togatas, Holeyas and Madigas. In the first part we give the economic profile of five villages selected on a random basis from the area in order to have a picture of the power relations among the castes resulting from material factors such as land, education and employment. It is based on a survey we have conducted ourselves. The picture of the economic conditions of different caste groups that emerges from this survey is a fair representation of the general situation in the whole area.

In the second part we describe the religio-cultural factors based on observations gathered from 20 villages in the area. The purpose of the study, as we mentioned above, is to understand the social role of these factors in terms of power relations among the castes.

From the above tables we find that 75.9% of total land is in the hands of the Reddy, Vokkaliga and Nagaratha Shetty communities. These communities also possess the maximum number of borewells and pumps, bullock carts, draught animals, milk cows, sheep and goats. Out of the total amount of loan of Rs. 4,52,429, from banks and farmers' co-operative service societies—this well-to-do section of the population has claimed Rs. 2,50,800 (55.43%). Apart from these agricultural forms of wealth, some families among them own power looms, flour mills, lorries, cars and two-wheelers.

Among the Brahmins only two families have land. The other two families support themselves with income from the priesthood and government jobs (cfr table 5 below).

Among the Tigalas, 52 people support themselves with small vegetable gardens, another 24 work also as wage labourers to supplement their income from land, while twenty of them have no land and depend entirely on wage labour. The 45 families of occupational castes like Ganigas and Kumbaras who are carpenters, potters, blacksmiths, goldsmiths, barbers etc., possess the least amount of land (0.9%). Most of them live by their vocational skills (cfr. table 5), others by agriculture and the rest combine agriculture and wage labour.

In the 152 families of Holeyas and Madigas, the so-called untouchables, quite a high number 93 persons, work on their own land and supplement their income with wage labour; 38 persons depend entirely on wage labour and only 26 are fully self-employed on their own farms.

From the above data it is clear that the economic power in the villages rests with the Reddy-Vokkaliga-Shetty communities. The vocational castes of Ganiga, Kumbara etc. depend on and serve these castes. The Tigalas, who grow vegetables, though comparatively poor, are not considered untouchables and have a status as small agriculturists. It is the Holeyas and Madigas who have the general image of agricultural wage labour.

Let us look at the educational and non-agricultural job pattern.

The educational and job patterns generally reflect the economic base of the respective groups. The education and jobs further strengthen the powerful position of the Reddys, Vokkaligas, Nagaratha Shettys and Brahmins. Let us now look at the religio-cultural scene of these villages, the data for which has been collected from twenty villages in Sarjapur Hobli in order to make sure that the religious scene presented here is representative of the area.

## Part II. Religio-Cultural Profile of the Villages

### I. Physical Exclusion of the Holeyas and Madigas from the Village Community

In the twenty villages studied, Holeyas are present in 19 where there are also caste Hindus; one small village is made up only of Holeyas. In the 19 villages, Holeyas live in a segregated area, separate from the rest. Madigas are found in six villages, and they live separate from both the caste Hindus and Holeyas.

In recent years there has been some change in this set-up. In ten villages the Janata Housing Scheme has been implemented. In four of these 10 villages, houses have been allotted without regard to caste. In another four villages Janata houses have been built in a separate area for the Holeyas only. In two villages Madigas have been given Janata houses in a separate area.

In most of the villages the caste Hindus themselves live in particular sectors according to their caste and Brahmins or the landlord castes like Reddy and Vokkaligas are found in the centre of the village. The major exception is a large newly developing village, Dodda Dommasandra, where houses are not segregated according to caste but found mingled.

### II. Devasthanams—Symbols of Caste Power

The physical territorial divide between the caste Hindus and the Holeyas and Madigas is culturally reflected in the nature of the places of worship in these villages. The caste Hindus and the Holeyas and Madigas have their own separate places of worship. These places mirror the power and the powerlessness of these two groups.

In the twenty villages surveyed by us, all the thirty important Devasthanams are places of worship of the caste Hindus, and the Holeyas and Madigas can make offerings to the gods in them only from outside. Twenty out of these thirty Devasthanams have land or other sources of income. 90%, that is, 18 out of 20 of these propertied temples have Brahmins or Lingayats as priests, and the remaining two have a Reddy and a Balajika as priest. There are 3 more Devasthanams where the priests are from castes such as Kumbara, Devanga and Tigala, but these 3 Devasthanams have no property. In all, 25 out of 30 Devasthanams have Brahmins or Lingayats as priests, though there are only a few families of Brahmins in these 20 villages.

The gods worshipped in these Devasthanams are all male gods, except one. They are Anjaneya, Veerabhadraswami, Gopalswami, Vinayaka, Gangadhareshwara, Basaveswara, Iswara, Lakshmi Narayana, Tirumalaswami, Pandurangaswami, names suggesting dominance of Vaishnava tradition. A god, Muneswara, worshipped by the so-called lower castes in their gudis with blood offerings is the principal deity in one of the Devasthanams where he is worshipped with vegetarian offerings. The only principal deity who is a female in Devasthanam is Muthelamma.

In contrast to the Devasthanams, the Holeyas and Madigas have as places of worship the gudis. These gudis are either an anthill or a crude image with or without a small roof over it. Some gudis are in a portion of the priest's dwelling. We have counted 29 such gudis in these 20 villages, belonging either to the Holeyas or Madigas. Generally a mother goddess under various names such as Yellamma, Kaveramma, Gangamma, Maduramma, Dugalamma, Maheswaramma, Pajamma, Dalamma, etc. is worshipped there. The only male god among them is the god Muneswara. Holeyas and Madigas have priests of their own community.



The caste Hindus also have their own *gudis* where they worship generally mother goddesses or Muneswara. But priests in these caste Hindu *gudis* are from professional castes such as Kumbara, Tigala, Achari, Togata (Devanga) and Kuruba and not Brahmin or Lingayat. Besides the *gudis*, every village has at least one snake shrine called *rache* which has to be installed by a Brahmin priest. In the two villages Holeyas and Madigas have made their own *rache* installed by their own priests.

### III. The Ambivalent Nature of Village Festivals

The religious exclusion of the Holeyas and Madigas from the routine religious life of the caste Hindus is broken only when caste Hindus come closer to the Holeyas and Madigas during the celebration of village festivals. In fact the roles played by the Holeyas and Madigas in some of the village festivals is quite prominent, and the worship is autochthonous and not brahminical. This is clear from the blood sacrifice and the priestly role played by a landlord or a low-casteman. It may be that these agricultural festivals of a matriarchal form of worship were the tradition of a non-caste people before the brahminical religious culture came to be associated with them in a semi feudal agricultural society. We can therefore divide the village festivals into two broad categories: ancient autochthonous festivals of the little tradition and brahminical festivals of the great tradition. Ancient autochthonous festivals of the people were made to reflect the feudal character and caste value of the Brahmins.

#### A. Prostitution of Autochthonous Festivals by the Caste Society

##### (a) Makara Sankranti

This festival occurs on 14th January every year. The Thoti, the representative head of the Holeyas<sup id="ref-1"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>, decorates the entrance of the village with a festoon of mango and neem tree leaves. It is also his duty to get ready a temporary place of worship a quarter of a kilometre away from the village under a mango or banyan tree. The place is cleaned with cowdung water.

The villagers gather there with decorated cattle. In some villages he-goats and rams are also taken. Bulls and cows which are wild and kicking are decorated with flowers, kumkum and turmeric powder, and packets of eatables are tied to them. The people erect a small shed with mango and neem leaves. A god called 'Katam Raju' (forest god) is installed inside the shed by arranging two rows of five small stones facing each other. One of the landlords or a potter by profession also plays the role of a priest. Nuts, fruits, vegetables, cooked rice, milk and milk products are placed in front of Katam Raju along with kumkum, turmeric, betel leaves and udubathi (agarbathi). Rice and milk are cooked and fowls are sacrificed before Katam Raju as part of the worship. Milk is boiled in a small earthen pot in front of the god. As the milk reaches the boiling point and is about to spill over, the Thoti breaks the pot, which is indeed a priestly role. In some villages the pot is not broken and people eagerly watch in which direction the milk spills over first. It is believed the direction in which the milk flows first will be safe for cattle in that year. The rice cooked before Katam Raju is distributed by the Thoti to every house for sprinkling over the cattle. The fowls are sacrificed after the milk-boiling rituals.<sup id="ref-2"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

When the worship is over the cattle are lined up around the temporary shrine and the priest sprinkles holy water over them. The procession back to the village starts in the evening, led by the land-owning castes such as Reddys and Vokkaligas with their cattle. They are followed by professional castes such as Kumbara (potters), Ganiga (oil-presser), Achari (carpenter) and Agasa (washermen). Holeyas and Madigas go at the end of the procession. When the procession arrives at the entrance of the village, the cattle are made to walk over the ashes of burnt straw of paddy and ragi. It is the job of the Thoti to collect straw from farmers, spread it over the road leading to the village and have it burnt. The valour of the village youth is to be proved at this time. As soon as the cattle cross the festoons anyone who has the courage to do so can try to take away the eatables tied to the cows and bulls.

After all the cattle have passed through the festoons, the Thoti cuts the festoons. The cattle are thereafter taken to the main temple in the village for worship. From the temple the procession goes through

#### (b) Agate Male Ungalu (Hasta Malai)

The festival of Agate Male Ungalu or Pongalu is observed during the time of Agate rain. A meeting of the villagers takes place one week prior to the festival and the date of the festival is decided during the meeting.<sup id="ref-3"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> The Thoti goes around the village collecting money from every household for the celebration. On the festival day the Thoti spreads ashes all around the boundary of the village land. Traditionally it is the duty of the Thoti to spread the ash over each and every family's fields. But these days some of the farmers do it themselves. People also draw figures of domestic animals and agricultural tools on the road with ashes. The Thoti digs a small pit at the entrance of the village. The pit is then filled with tender plants of ragi, paddy, jawar and other crops grown in the village. The village headman, along with others, comes to the pit with a ram; it is cut and the blood is poured over the plants in the pit. Some of the blood-soaked plants are taken by the farmers and thrown over the agricultural fields in the village. The head of the sheep is given to the Thoti.

If there are Brahmins and Lingayats in the village they do not take part in the ritual, but only contribute money towards the expenses. The ceremony is done without the usual drum or pipe music.

#### (c) Gangamma festival

The Gangamma festival is celebrated in a village when the water tank gets full in any year. Money is collected from all the villagers and a buffalo is bought for the occasion and taken in a procession to the village tank. The procession is led by Reddys, Vokkaligas, Kumbaras, Agasas, Hajamas, Acharis and others in front with lamps, and the Holeyas and Madigas follow at a little distance. A boat (tappa) made out of plantain trunk by the Thoti of the village is floated in the tank with lamps in it. The buffalo is sacrificed near the tank and the meat is distributed among the Holeyas.

The priest, if he happens to be a Brahmin or Lingayat, performs the puja and leaves the place prior to the animal sacrifice. The drum is beaten by the village Thoti and the pipe is played by the caste Hindu or Holeya barber.

#### (d) Sapplamma festival

This festival which has the status of *Ur habba* or village festival in this area, is that of the goddess Sapplamma, believed to be one of seven sisters. The belief in seven mother goddesses who are sisters is very common in the villages of South India.<sup id="ref-4"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

The villages in this area bring the festival idol (*utsava moorti*) of Sapplamma from a village called Tornahally, which is outside the region, near Malur town. Tornahally is the venue of the weekly market and annual Jathra when thousands of people go there with their cattle for sale. The priests of the Sapplamma temple belong to a family of the Tigala community who are engaged in growing vegetables in and around Bangalore. This temple at present comes under the control of the Muzrai Department.

According to the present head priest, Venkataramanappa, the Sapplamma of Tornahally had been brought from the Sapplamma temple built by the Ganga dynasty near Chikkaballapura. The story goes that about one hundred and fifty years ago the villagers in the Tornahally region used to visit Sapplamma in Chikkaballapura as they visit her these days in Tornahally for safety from rinderpest and other cattle diseases. A certain Lingyat man from Tornahally visited the Sapplamma temple in Chikkaballapura, and returned to the village with kumkum and turmeric powder. On his return, he found the kumkum and turmeric powder had turned into a beautiful wedding necklace. This was reported to one of the forefathers of the present priest. On his advice, the villagers agreed to return the necklace immediately to the goddess. The following day when the villagers got ready to travel to Chikkaballapura, no one was able to move including the bullocks ploughing the field. When the villagers gathered to discuss the strange happening, a boy became possessed by the goddess Sapplamma and declared that the goddess had come to the village on her own in the form of the necklace. An anthill was pointed out where they should build a temple for her. The possessed boy also pointed out a small boy who was to be her priest. When the people protested that the chosen priest was a left-handed boy, the goddess indicated that she was happy with a left-handed priest. This is how the Tigala family got the priesthood and the present priest believes that he is the 7th in line. Sapplamma, according to the belief of the people, lives inside the anthill—inside the *garbagudi* (sanctorum) of the temple.

The following procedure is observed for bringing the goddess Sapplamma to the villages under study. A small delegation from the village, usually of the landlord castes, goes to Tornahally to take the permission of the goddess for their festival. A flower is put on the goddess' head. If the flower falls from the head to its right side permission is given. The request can be repeated thrice. If the answer is in the negative, the delegation excuses itself and returns home, hoping to come back the next year. The consent can also be given by the goddess through the possessed priest. The village which gets permission can collect the *utsava* (procession) idol which will be in one of the nearby villages on its rounds from one village to the other. If the festival date of a village coincides with that of another village, they have to make do with the holy water brought from Tornahally.

The goddess is brought from another village in a procession. Every head of the family in the village goes for this purpose. The Thoti of the village beats the drum. The goddess is brought and kept on the eastern side of the village, about fifty or hundred yards away. Wherever the Brahmin and Lingayat community members are present, they worship the goddess first by breaking a big *kumbalakayee* (pumpkin) before the animal sacrifice begins. They are followed by the caste Hindus offering a sheep. The head of the sheep is given to the priest. The Holeyas and Madigas offer a buffalo and its head is given to the priest who in turn gives it to the thoti for his services.

In some villages Holeyas and Madigas are not allowed to carry the goddess and the procession does not go through their streets. However, according to the priest, the goddess is for all castes, though he cannot do anything against people practising caste discrimination.

When the goddess leaves the village after the celebration, offerings of sari, blouse-piece and other things are made to her after the fashion of a daughter leaving her father's house.
