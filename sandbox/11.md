**"Flaws in the UIDAI Process"** is an essay by Hans Verghese Mathews, published in *Economic & Political Weekly* on 27 February 2016 (Vol. LI, No. 9, pp. 74–78). The article examines whether the biometric identification process used by the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) could reliably distinguish individuals across a population the size of India's. Using results from an early UIDAI experiment and elementary probability calculations, Mathews estimates the expected number of people whose biometric identifiers would match those of at least one other person. His central finding is that the proportion of such people, which he calls "duplicands", would be too high for the system to serve as a reliable means of uniquely identifying individuals.

The PDF supplied for this research record contains the five-page journal article (printed pages 74–78) followed by a four-page technical supplement. The supplement develops mathematical bounds used in the article.

## Abstract

The article evaluates the risk of false-positive matches in the composite biometric identifier used by UIDAI. A false positive occurs when the biometric identifiers of two different people are judged to match. Mathews calls a person whose identifier matches that of at least one other person a "duplicand".

The analysis starts with an estimate of the probability of a false positive from an experiment reported by UIDAI. It then uses probability bounds and estimates of the number of matching pairs and groups to calculate how many enrolled people might be duplicands. For a population of 1.2 billion, the article estimates approximately 9.92 million duplicands, or about one in every 121 people. For a population of 1.5 billion, the estimate rises to approximately 15.51 million, or about one in every 97 people.

The paper argues that these proportions are incompatible with the claim that the biometric system can uniquely identify individuals reliably.

## Context and problem

UIDAI was engaged in biometrically identifying India's population and issuing Aadhaar numbers intended to act as unique identifiers in transactions with the state. The system described in the paper combines fingerprints and iris images to generate a composite biometric identifier for each individual.

The article distinguishes the biometric identifier from the Aadhaar number itself. Its concern is not how the number is generated, but whether the composite biometric identifier can distinguish one person from every other person in a very large population.

A biometric device does not necessarily produce an identical numerical representation every time the same physical feature is presented. A system therefore needs a matching rule: two representations are treated as a match when the calculated distance between them does not exceed a specified threshold. That threshold is determined experimentally. Even when the people being compared are different, their representations may fall within the matching threshold. This is the false-positive problem.

Mathews uses the term "organic source" for the person from whom a biometric identifier originates. Two identifiers may match even though their organic sources are different. The paper calls such people duplicands when their identifiers match those of at least one other enrolled person.

## The mathematical model

Let ξ (xi) denote the probability that the biometric identifiers of two different people produce a false-positive match. The paper initially considers a new enrollee whose identifier is compared with the identifiers of k people already enrolled.

If each comparison has the same false-positive probability ξ, and the match events are assumed to be independent, the probability that the new identifier matches none of the k stored templates is:

<div class="math-display">

\[
1-\Phi(k)=(1-\xi)^k
\tag{1}
\]

</div>

Here, Φ(k) is the probability that the new identifier matches at least one of the k stored templates. The paper derives bounds that make ξ estimable without having to calculate a difficult large root:

<div class="math-display">

\[
\frac{\Phi(k)}{k}
\leq \xi
\leq
\frac{-\log[1-\Phi(k)]}{k}
\tag{2}
\]

</div>

Once n distinct people have been enrolled, there are n(n − 1)/2 distinct pairs of identifiers. The expected number of matching pairs is therefore:

<div class="math-display">

\[
M(n)=\xi\,\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
\tag{3}
\]

</div>

The number of matching pairs is not automatically the same as the number of duplicands. A person may match more than one other person, and matches may form groups of three or more. The article therefore develops a further calculation to estimate the number of distinct people involved in matches, rather than simply doubling the number of matching pairs.

For a new identifier compared against k stored templates, the probability of matching at least q of them is denoted by Φq(k). The paper expresses this probability using the binomial distribution:

<div class="math-display">

\[
\Phi_q(k)=
1-\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}
\binom{k}{r}\xi^r(1-\xi)^{k-r}
\tag{4}
\]

</div>

The article then develops workable bounds for these probabilities. It uses them to estimate the number of identifiers that match one or more predecessors, and the number that match two or more. The latter counts help correct for the possibility that a group of matching identifiers would otherwise be counted more than once.

## The UIDAI experiment and estimate

The paper relies on an experiment reported by UIDAI in which four million trials were conducted when approximately 84 million people had been enrolled. In each trial, one stored template was selected and compared against the remainder to determine whether a match occurred.

The report recorded 2,309 matches in four million trials. Mathews uses this result as an estimate of Φ1(k), the probability that a selected template matches at least one of the other stored templates. Applying the bounds in equation (2), he obtains the following estimate for the false-positive probability of the composite biometric identifier:

<div class="math-display">

\[
0.687202381\times10^{-11}
\leq \xi \leq
0.6874008011\times10^{-11}
\tag{7}
\]

</div>

The article's argument is that UIDAI could have used the results of its own early experiment to estimate the false-positive probability and, from that, the likely scale of duplicands as enrolment expanded.

The calculations assume that match events between different stored templates occur independently. The paper also considers the possibility of people attempting to enrol more than once, but presents the central estimate as applying even under the "best of circumstances", when no one attempts multiple enrolments.

## Conclusions and significance

Mathews concludes that the estimated proportions of duplicands are too high for the programme to reliably distinguish every individual. At the population level considered current in the article, the estimate is approximately one duplicand in every 121 people. At 1.5 billion enrolled people, it becomes approximately one in every 97.

The central criticism is methodological and quantitative: the early UIDAI experiment contained information from which the authority could have estimated the false-positive probability and projected the likely number of matching identifiers as enrolment expanded. The paper argues that this elementary calculation should have been performed and considered when assessing the system's ability to provide unique identification.

The conclusion is specifically about the statistical reliability of the composite biometric identifier under the model and assumptions developed in the article. It should not be read as a claim that the paper individually identifies actual duplicands or documents the later operational history of Aadhaar.

## Technical supplement

The supplied PDF includes a technical supplement after the journal article. Its opening note says that it retains the paper's notation for convenience, while presenting self-contained arguments that may be read independently.

The supplement develops the inequalities used in the main article. It begins with the relationship:

<div class="math-display">

\[
\Phi(n)=1-(1-\xi)^n
\]

</div>

It then derives bounds relating Φ(n) and ξ, establishes bounds for the probability of multiple matches, and uses the incomplete beta function to obtain the upper bound required for the article's equation (6). The later pages estimate the higher-order terms Tq(N), explaining why terms beyond the fourth order can be treated as zero for the numerical range considered.

The supplement is mathematically important because it provides the derivations behind the bounds used in the article rather than asking readers to accept those bounds without proof.
