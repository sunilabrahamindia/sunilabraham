# Simulation #3: The Internet Governance Policy Laboratory

How do decision-making rules shape whose voices count in internet governance? This interactive policy laboratory lets you change a fictional internet policy proposal, adjust the institutional rules, and compare the results. It is designed as a transparent thought experiment, not as a prediction of how real negotiations will turn out.

      <h3>Model specification and limits</h3>
      <details>
        <summary>How are stakeholder preferences represented?</summary>
        <div class="ig-detail-body">
          <p>Each constituency is assigned an illustrative preferred value for each of the four policy dimensions, plus a weight showing how important that dimension is in its modelled position. Preference fit starts at 100 and decreases with the weighted distance between the proposal and that constituency's preferred values.</p>
          <p>The preferences are transparent assumptions created for this educational experiment. They are not derived from interviews, surveys, official positions or a representative sample. Changing them would change the results. The model intentionally does not claim that a constituency has one real-world view.</p>
          <div class="ig-table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Constituency</th><th>Transparency</th><th>Due process</th><th>Government discretion</th><th>Safeguards</th><th>Dimension weights (in the same order)</th></tr></thead><tbody>
            <tr><td>Governments</td><td>55</td><td>50</td><td>75</td><td>60</td><td>15 / 15 / 50 / 20</td></tr>
            <tr><td>Platforms and providers</td><td>65</td><td>60</td><td>50</td><td>70</td><td>20 / 15 / 25 / 40</td></tr>
            <tr><td>Civil society</td><td>90</td><td>90</td><td>25</td><td>80</td><td>25 / 35 / 10 / 30</td></tr>
            <tr><td>Technical community</td><td>65</td><td>60</td><td>45</td><td>90</td><td>15 / 10 / 15 / 60</td></tr>
            <tr><td>Affected users and communities</td><td>85</td><td>85</td><td>30</td><td>70</td><td>25 / 35 / 10 / 30</td></tr>
          </tbody></table></div>
          <p>These numbers are deliberately visible so that readers can challenge them. They should not be mistaken for empirically established stakeholder preferences.</p>
        </div>
      </details>
      <details>
        <summary>How is participation estimated?</summary>
        <div class="ig-detail-body">
          <p>Each constituency has a declared baseline capacity: governments 90, platforms 95, civil society 55, technical community 65, and affected users and communities 35. The model combines 55% of the participation-access setting with 45% of baseline capacity. Support for under-resourced groups adds a targeted boost to civil society and affected communities, with the boost scaled by how limited their baseline capacity is.</p>
          <p>This is a transparent teaching mechanism, not a measured attendance forecast. A group counts as participating at a modelled participation level of 30 or more. Lowering access can therefore reduce the number of groups represented, while capacity support can reduce—but does not automatically erase—the gap.</p>
        </div>
      </details>
      <details>
        <summary>What exactly do the three decision rules mean here?</summary>
        <div class="ig-detail-body">
          <ul>
            <li><strong>Rough-consensus seeking:</strong> requires at least three participating constituencies, mean preference fit of at least 60, at least three participating groups with fit of 55 or more, and no more than one participating group with fit below 30. This is a simplified operational proxy for exploring consensus-seeking; it is not the formal procedure of the IGF, IETF or ICANN.</li>
            <li><strong>Equal-constituency majority:</strong> each participating constituency is one vote. A constituency votes in favour when its preference fit is 55 or more. A simple majority of participating constituencies is required.</li>
            <li><strong>Weighted influence vote:</strong> the illustrative base weights are governments 28, platforms 26, civil society 20, technical community 14, and affected users and communities 12. Each weight is multiplied by the constituency's modelled participation level. The proposal passes when at least half of the participating effective weight supports it. These weights are a deliberately contestable scenario, not a description of any real institution.</li>
          </ul>
          <p>The three rules are comparison devices. Actual institutions have their own mandates, membership, procedural rules, appeal mechanisms and definitions of consensus.</p>
        </div>
      </details>
      <details>
        <summary>How is the accountability safeguards proxy calculated?</summary>
        <div class="ig-detail-body"><p>The proxy is 40% process transparency, 35% policy transparency and 25% due process. The weights are design choices for this teaching model, not a validated scale. It is displayed separately from preference fit and does not directly determine whether a proposal passes. Visitors should challenge the weights and can observe how changing any of the three inputs changes this proxy.</p></div>
      </details>
      <details>
        <summary>How is influence concentration calculated?</summary>
        <div class="ig-detail-body">
          <p>The model calculates a Herfindahl-style concentration index from effective influence shares. It sums the squared shares of participating groups and multiplies by 100. If five groups have exactly equal effective shares, the index is 20; if one group holds all effective influence, it is 100. This is a mathematical description of the model's assumed distribution, not a normative verdict by itself. For the majority and rough-consensus rules, the vote outcome does not use these influence weights.</p>
        </div>
      </details>
      <details>
        <summary>What this model cannot tell us</summary>
        <div class="ig-detail-body">
          <ul>
            <li>It cannot predict how a real summit, treaty process, standards body or national regulator will behave.</li>
            <li>It does not model strategic bargaining, changing alliances, legal authority, informal networks, historical context or the full diversity within each constituency.</li>
            <li>It does not measure actual human rights outcomes, technical security, implementation success or public legitimacy. The results are outputs of declared assumptions.</li>
            <li>It does not establish that one governance model is always superior. A different scenario, set of preferences or institutional mandate can produce different outcomes.</li>
          </ul>
          <p>The proper use is to formulate questions, inspect mechanisms and compare assumptions—not to treat the output as empirical evidence.</p>
        </div>
      </details>

## How to use the laboratory

Start with the balanced starting proposal and run the experiment. Then change only the decision rule and run it again. Next, reduce participation access, or increase support for under-resourced groups, and observe how the number of participating constituencies changes. Save up to three runs to compare the results.

A useful question to investigate is whether a proposal that passes a voting rule also has broad support among the groups affected by it. The model can make that difference visible, but it cannot decide by itself whether a policy is legitimate or just.
