The Christian Institute for the Study of Religion and Society (CISRS) expresses its immense gratitude and enthusiasm for the commendable initiative taken by the family members of Fr. Athanasius Mathen Abraham Ayrookuzhiel (A. M. A. Ayrookuzhiel) to compile and preserve his unpublished articles and essays. Fr. Ayrookuzhiel served as Associate Director of CISRS from 1973 to 1996, playing a pivotal role alongside Dr. M.M. Thomas, the then Director, in envisioning novel studies in the field of religion, which was the prime concern of CISRS at that time. Fr Abraham Ayrookuzhiel brought a wealth of scholarly knowledge to the institute, having studied religion in prestigious institutions in Rome and Oxford.

His dedication to the study of religion was particularly evident when he responded to the call of the 1973 biennial council of CISRS, which emphasized the importance of addressing the religious dynamics affecting the marginalized sections of society (Ayrookuzhiel calls it “popular religions”) within the broader context of “humanisation.” One of his most notable contributions was the initiation of a groundbreaking project to study the religious experiences of the Dalit community in Chirakal village in north Kerala. This project not only transformed the approach taken by CISRS but also significantly influenced the theological landscape in India, signaling a paradigm shift in the understanding of religion and social justice. It is important to note that the paradigm shift he introduced represented a departure from the approaches of P.D. Devanandan and M.M. Thomas—the pioneers of CISRS—who primarily focused on Hindu-Vedic religions in their efforts to initiate a theology of interfaith dialogue in India.

The essays gathered in this volume serve as a continuation of the scholarly work published by CISRS in 2006, aptly titled *Essays on Dalits, Religion and Liberation*. CISRS has been instrumental in disseminating a substantial body of Ayrookuzhiel’s scholarly books and essays, primarily through its esteemed journal, *Religion & Society*. Among the pivotal works that underscore the scholarly contribution of the institute are *The Sacred in Popular Hinduism* (1983), *Swami Anand Thirth: Untouchability, Gandhian Solution on Trial* (1987), *The Dalit Deśiyata: The Kerala Experience in Development and Class Struggle* (1990, editor), *Dalit Kavithakal: Oru Padanam* (1992, co-editor), *Dalit Sahityam* (1995, co-author), and *Essays on Dalits, Religion and Liberation* (2006). These texts collectively enrich the discourse surrounding the religions of marginalized communities, cementing CISRS' pivotal role as an authority on the study of religion at the intersection of social exclusion and marginalization.

CISRS pays tribute to one of the pioneering figures whose life was dedicated to advocating for the rights and dignity of Dalits, the most disenfranchised communities in our society. His profound insights and research have infused a deeper significance into the mission of CISRS, a Christian research institute that emphasizes the complex interplay between religion and societal structures. This commitment is particularly directed toward those who inhabit the fringes of faith and seek to reinterpret scripture, symbols, and liturgical practices as divine answers to the urgent cries of Dalit communities advocating for their rightful recognition and justice.

Moreover, the contributions of this scholar to the Christian landscape in India resonate deeply with the revolutionary spirit of B.R. Ambedkar, who reinterpreted Buddhism as a Navayana, or reformed, tradition. This legacy calls for the envisioning of a Navayana Christianity—an indigenous spiritual path that takes a radical shift from the Eurocentric framework and is intertwined with the struggles of the marginalized, empowering them in their journey toward transformation and self-realization within the context of faith.

This volume presents a compelling collection of significant essays by Abraham Ayrookuzhiel that were not included in the earlier volume. Central to these essays is the endeavor to construct an authentic theology and ideology aimed at Dalit liberation. Ayrookuzhiel boldly critiques the transcendent and universalist perspectives typically associated with Kantian and Hegelian philosophies of religion. Instead, he engages with the Feuerbachian and Ambedkarite frameworks, which position religion as an ideology serving the interests of the powerful. However, he takes a transformative stance, proposing that religion can also represent a radical movement for the marginalized—those who craft counter-theologies and liturgies to fulfill their aspirations of becoming authentic human beings.

In his analysis, Ayrookuzhiel contends that any Dalit theology and ideology emerging in India cannot find its foundation in the prevailing Brahminical-casteist social order. This order, he argues, has historically perpetuated systems of hierarchy, pollution, exclusion, and social powerlessness. Rather, he seeks to identify the resources for Dalit theology within the rich tapestry of Dalit folk traditions, protest cultures, alternative spiritual currents, and historical movements that have actively challenged the dominance of caste.

Engaging with Dalit communities in north Kerala, he explores their personal aspirations and their perceptions of religion's role in relation to their dreams for liberation. Ayrookuzhiel observes that, while aspirations for freedom, equality, fraternity, and social justice resonate widely, many individuals do not view religion as a pivotal force in achieving these goals. He examines the implications of these views for religious practices, the scholarly study of Hinduism, and the dynamics of Hindu-Christian dialogue in modern India.

Ayrookuzhiel’s approach deserves recognition for its emphasis on grounding religion in the lived experiences of the marginalized. He reveals how they envision a newfound existence characterized by freedom and dignity while simultaneously critiquing the oppressive facets of religion. This is vividly illustrated through his empirical research conducted in Sarjapura Hobli within the Anekal Taluk of Bengaluru district. He examines the relationship between village religion and the allocation of economic and social power, showing how these elements contribute to legitimizing the caste system and the accompanying social, economic, and political hierarchies.

Challenging the conventional methods of Hindu-Christian interfaith dialogue, which often rest on specific philosophical principles, Ayrookuzhiel calls for a radical re-examination. He urges a focus on how common people articulate their religious beliefs, encounter divine power, comprehend moral values, and interpret rituals and sacred symbols in their daily lives. His advocacy for empirical and critical approaches to the study of religion and interfaith discourse seeks to illuminate the intricate connections between faith, justice, and human liberation.

Furthermore, he encourages churches in India to engage in more meaningful dialogues with the lived religious experiences of ordinary people, especially the marginalized Dalit communities, and to reimagine theology in light of their aspirations for “humanisation.” In this context, Ayrookuzhiel does not merely propose an innovative empirical methodology for studying religion; he provides a framework that fosters interreligious dialogue and a theology that fosters counter-ideological practices, ensuring freedom, dignity, and fraternity in the everyday realities of life.

A particularly noteworthy aspect of Ayrookuzhiel’s writings is his vision of a new form of sacredness that is both immanent and imminent. He conceptualizes a sacred being—a God—who exists alongside people, speaks in the voice of those struggling, and walks with them toward a hopeful reality where justice flows abundantly and all individuals coexist in a culture of fraternity. This notion of sacredness, referred to as "Sacrafanations" in the title of CISRS’ festschrift honoring Ayrookuzhiel (2023), interweaves and intertwines the sacred with the profane, rejecting the dichotomy of the two in favor of their interconnectedness, as expressed through the socio-political aspirations of the former untouchables—the Dalits.

When Ayrookuzhiel refers to God as Potten Theyyam, drawing on a Dalit counter-religious tradition in northern Kerala, he envisions the sacred as an embodiment of immanent *shakti*—energy—a reflection of the collective vulnerability that persists. He reminds us of the critical insights offered by contemporary radical materialist traditions in religion, particularly the thoughts of Giorgio Agamben, a contemporary Italian philosopher and theologian, who famously asserted that “profane is my God” (1993:90).[^1] Through this lens, Ayrookuzhiel encourages a re-evaluation of the relationship between the divine and the struggles of the marginalized, emphasizing the transformative potential of this connection.

Ayrookuzhiel draws attention to the poignant song of Potten Theyyam, a revered symbol within the Dalit community. The term "Potten," which translates to "fool," when combined with "Theyyam," conveys the notion of the "divine fool." This intriguing concept embodies an apparent foolishness that hides profound theological insights. Theyyam itself represents a vibrant performance art that finds its roots in the term "Daivam," meaning God. This etymology highlights a significant theological perspective: the sacred is intricately linked to the inherent energy of resistance among the marginalized, rather than being seen as a transcendent reality who comes to the world to offer salvation.

In this spirit, the CISRS takes great pride in introducing the profound insights shared by our visionary pioneer. He laid the groundwork for an egalitarian ideology and theological framework that serves as the foundation for our institute.
