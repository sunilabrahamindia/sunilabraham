**"A Privacy Meeting with the Federal Trade Commission in New Delhi"** is a post by [Elonnai Hickok](/elonnai/) published by the [Centre for Internet and Society](/cis/) on 30 September 2013. It recounts a roundtable meeting held between CIS and two Federal Trade Commission officials, Betsy Broder and Sarah Schroeder, in New Delhi, discussing the sectoral US approach to privacy regulation and potential frameworks and challenges for privacy in India.

## Full Text

<div class="highlighted-text" id="fulltext">

<p>On September 20, the Centre for Internet and Society held a roundtable meeting with Betsy Broder, Counsel for International Consumer Protection, and Sarah Schroeder, Attorney, Bureau of Consumer Protection, Federal Trade Commission (FTC), United States. The meeting took place at the Imperial, Janpath, New Delhi and discussed both the U.S framework to privacy and potential frameworks and challenges to privacy in India.</p>

<hr class="article-separator">

<p><em>As a note, thoughts shared during the meeting represented personal perspectives, and did not constitute the official position of the Federal Trade Commission.</em></p>

<p>When explaining the U.S regulatory framework for privacy the FTC attorneys highlighted that the United States does not have comprehensive privacy legislation, like in Europe, but instead has sectoral laws that address different aspects of privacy. For example, the Fair Credit Reporting Act maintains confidentiality of consumer credit report information, the Gramm Leach Bliley Act imposes privacy and security requirements for financial institutions, HIPAA applies to patient health information, and the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act prevents the collection and posting of personal information from minors. It was discussed that the sectoral model followed by the United States allows for a nuanced balance to be struck between privacy protection and the market. It was noted, however, that some have critiqued the U.S. regulatory framework for lacking clear principles that apply to the commercial world and lay out strong privacy protections for the individual. In light of this, the White House is developing a Privacy Bill of Rights.</p>

<p>The Federal Trade Commission is an independent agency in the United States Government with responsibility for enforcing both consumer protection and competition laws. It is composed of five commissioners, and a staff of roughly 1,000, which includes attorneys and economists. The FTC is primarily a law enforcement agency, but also undertakes policy development through workshops and reports, Consumer education is another key function of the agency.</p>

<p>On the consumer protection side, Congress has directed the FTC to enforce the Federal Trade Commission Act, as well as some more specific statutes, such as those that protect consumers from unwanted telemarketing laws, and the protection of children on line. Its main objectives are to protect consumer interests, and prevent fraud and unfair and deceptive business practices. The FTC carries out its privacy work through its consumer protection mission.</p>

<p>When understanding the FTC's role in relation to privacy, it is important to understand that the FTC's jurisdiction applies only to certain industries as defined by Congress. Thus, for example, the FTC does not have jurisdiction over banks or telecommunications.</p>

<p>The most critical part of the FTC's activities is its law enforcement function. The FTC can investigate an organization if the staff believes that the entity may be involved in conduct that contravenes the FTC Act's prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices, or another specific privacy law. The FTC has brought a number of privacy-related cases against major companies including Facebook, Google, ChoicePoint, and Twitter. Many of these cases address new challenges brought about by rapidly changing technologies.</p>

<p>The vast majority of the FTC's actions have been settled with consent judgments. When the statute that the FTC enforces allows for the imposition of a civil penalty, the FTC sets the penalty at a level that ensures that it is fair and provides a deterrent, but will not impose a hardship on the company. As a civil enforcement agency, the FTC cannot seek criminal sanctions. While enforcement is the cornerstone of the FTC's approach to privacy, the agency also supports self-regulation, where appropriate. In this system the FTC does not pre-approve an organization's practices or define principles that all companies should abide by as it is felt that every organization is unique and has different needs and abilities, and assigning specific technical standards may stifle innovation.</p>

<p>In the meeting it was also discussed how US privacy laws may apply to overseas companies where they are providing services for US consumers or working on behalf of US companies. For example, under the Gramm Leach Bliley Act the FTC has created the Safeguards Rule, which speaks to how financial data by financial institutions must be handled and protected. This Rule applies to companies overseas if the company is performing work for US companies or US consumers. In other words, a US company cannot avoid compliance by outsourcing its work to an off shore organization.</p>

<p>Discussions during the meeting also focused on consent and the key role that context, accessibility, and timing play in ensuring individuals have the ability to provide informed consent. Some of the attendees suggested that this practice could be greatly improved in India. For example, currently in India there are companies that only provide consumers access to the company privacy policy after an individual has consented and signed up to the service. When asked about the challenges to privacy that exist in India, many shared that, culturally, there is a different understanding of privacy in India than in many western countries.</p>

<p>Other thoughts included that the Indian government is currently imagining privacy regulation as being either fluid and purely self regulatory or being enforced through strict legal provisions. Instead, the government needs to begin to expand the possibilities for a regulatory framework for privacy in India in such a way that allows for strong legal enforcement, and flexible standards. The right to be forgotten was also discussed and it was mentioned that California has proposed a law that will allow individuals to request deletion of information.</p>

## Context and Background

In September 2013, the Centre for Internet and Society (CIS) published Elonnai Hickok's account of a roundtable meeting held on 20 September at the Imperial hotel in New Delhi between CIS and two officials from the United States Federal Trade Commission (FTC): Betsy Broder, Counsel for International Consumer Protection, and Sarah Schroeder, an attorney with the Bureau of Consumer Protection. The post opens with a disclaimer that the views shared during the meeting were personal perspectives and did not represent the FTC's official position.

Much of the discussion centred on explaining how the United States regulates privacy through a sectoral rather than comprehensive legislative model, rather than through comprehensive privacy legislation of the kind described in the post in relation to Europe. The FTC officials pointed to targeted statutes such as the Fair Credit Reporting Act governing consumer credit data, the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act covering financial institutions, HIPAA for patient health information, and the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act restricting collection of minors' data. While this sectoral model was described as allowing a more nuanced balance between privacy protection and market flexibility, the post noted that critics have faulted it for lacking a unifying set of commercial privacy principles, a gap the White House was reportedly addressing at the time through a developing Privacy Bill of Rights.

The post also detailed the FTC's institutional structure and enforcement powers, describing it as an independent five-commissioner agency with a staff of roughly 1,000 that enforces the FTC Act's prohibition on unfair or deceptive business practices, alongside more specific statutes. It clarified that FTC jurisdiction excludes certain sectors entirely, such as banking and telecommunications, and that the agency has brought privacy enforcement actions against major companies including Facebook, Google, ChoicePoint and Twitter, typically resolved through consent judgments rather than criminal sanctions, since the FTC operates as a civil enforcement body. The meeting also touched on how US privacy rules can reach overseas companies, citing the Safeguards Rule under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, which, according to the discussion, applies to overseas companies performing work for US companies or consumers.

The conversation then turned to challenges specific to India, with attendees noting that Indian companies often provide access to privacy policies only after a user has already consented to a service, undermining meaningful informed consent, and that cultural understandings of privacy in India differ from those in many western countries. Participants observed that Indian privacy regulation was, at the time, largely conceived as a binary choice between loose self-regulation and strict legal enforcement, and argued for a middle path combining robust legal enforcement with flexible standards. The meeting also touched on the "right to be forgotten," referencing a then-proposed California law that would allow individuals to request deletion of their personal information.
